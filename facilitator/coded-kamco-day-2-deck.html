Topic 1 · Memo or deck? Then Word
The story
Fourteen slidesThe MD asked for “something for the Investment Committee”. You built fourteen beautiful slides. The committee reads every paper at home, the night before. They wanted one page. You start again at 9 pm.

The wrong format is the most expensive mistake.

Idea 1 of 2
The analogy

A letter or a speech.

A letter is read alone, at the reader's pace, and must make sense without you. A speech is heard once, with you in the room to explain. Different jobs, different shapes.
The term & the link

This is memo or deck.

MEMO OR DECK A memo (Word) when the reader reads alone, needs detail, or decides later. A deck (PowerPoint) when you present live to a group and pictures help.
Like the letter and the speech, ask how it will be read…
→
Who reads it? How? What decision?
The request usually tells you. Read it again before you build.

Idea 2 of 2
The analogy

The newspaper headline.

A newspaper puts the most important fact in the headline and the first line. If you stop reading after one sentence, you still know what happened.
The term & the link

This is bottom line up front.

BOTTOM LINE UP FRONT Start with the answer and the action in two sentences. Then give the evidence: performance, allocation, issuer check, movers.
Like the headline, the reader gets the point first…
→
the rest is evidence they can check.

Idea 1 of 2
The analogy

Road signs.

A road sign gives one message you can read at speed: “Exit 3 →”. Imagine a sign with nine lines of text. You would miss your exit.
The term & the link

This is one slide, one message.

ONE SLIDE, ONE MESSAGE Each slide title is a full sentence that states the message. The body is only the evidence for that sentence — a chart or two or three points.
Like the road sign, readable at speed…
→
if they read only the titles, they still get the story.

Idea 1 of 2
The analogy

The pilot's pre-flight checklist.

Pilots with twenty years of experience still use the checklist before every flight. Not because they forget — because the checklist catches what experience misses on a busy day.
The term & the link

These are the four checks.

THE FOUR CHECKS Accuracy: every number matches your analysis. Confidentiality: only this client, only the right people. Tone: fits the reader, no promises. Completeness: everything asked for, attachment, people copied, disclaimer if needed.
Like the checklist, every send, every time…
→
you sign the email, not Copilot.

Topic 4 · From manual to agent
The story
Every SundayEvery Sunday, Faisal's team receives forty client requests. Someone reads each one to find the client, the ask, the deadline and the risks. Same steps, every week — and every week someone misses a changed deadline.

A repeated job is a job for an agent.

Idea 1 of 1
The analogy

The job description.

When you hire someone, you write a job description: the role, the rules, the files they use, and what a good result looks like. Then you test them before you trust them.
The term & the link

This is an agent.

AGENT (COPILOT AGENT BUILDER) A version of Copilot set up for one repeated job, with its own instructions (the job description), knowledge (the files it uses) and starter prompts. You build it in the Microsoft 365 Copilot app with Create agent.
Like a job description, clear role, clear rules…
→
an agent is only as good as its instructions.
Automate the repeat. Keep the judgement.

Project · graded · 80 minutes

One request. Alone. Start to finish.

At 13:30 a new email arrives in your inbox — version A or version B. Treat it like a real request from a KAMCO manager.

  1. Read the email with Copilot in Outlook. Write down the deadline and the deliverable.
  2. Clean and analyse the attached custody export in Excel. Check every formula.
  3. Produce the deliverable: A = one-page Word memo · B = 5-slide PowerPoint deck.
  4. Reply to the email with your file attached by 2:50 pm. Add one line at the end: “What I corrected: …”
  5. Finished early? Build the other format too (A → a 3-slide deck, B → a one-page memo) — it is not graded, but it is great practice.
